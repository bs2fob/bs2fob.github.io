1. Three inferences, one bag of beans

In an 1878 essay, Charles Sanders Peirce split inference into three using a single bag of beans. There are three sentences to work with: a rule, all the beans from this bag are white; a case, these beans are from this bag; and a result, these beans are white. Which two you hold and which one you draw out decides what kind of inference you're making.

Hold the rule and the case, draw out the result, and you have deduction. The beans in the bag are all white, these came from the bag, so these are white. It can't go wrong, but you learn nothing new. The conclusion was already sitting inside the premises.

Hold the case and the result, draw out the rule, and you have induction. You took a handful from the bag and every one was white, so the whole bag is probably white. More samples make it more trustworthy, but it stays a probability to the end.

Hold the rule and the result, draw out the case, and you have abduction. There are white beans on the table and a bag of nothing but white beans beside them, so these beans probably came from that bag. It is the easiest of the three to get wrong. It is also the only one of the three that produces something that wasn't in front of you. The story linking the beans to the bag was written nowhere in what you observed. Abduction is what puts that story up in the first place.

2. Only abduction opens a hypothesis space

Lay the three side by side and an order appears. Deduction unfolds things formally inside a hypothesis space that has already been set up, and induction attaches probabilistic confidence to what comes out of that space. Both need to know what they're examining before they can move. Deciding what to examine — setting up a new hypothesis space in the first place — is something only abduction does.

So the relation among the three is not a ranking of value but an order of operation. If abduction doesn't open a space, deduction has nothing to unfold and induction has nowhere to converge. Deduction and induction are auxiliary operations that only run on territory abduction has already cleared.

Put more concretely, abduction is catching a mismatch in the noise and seating it in the right place on your mental map. Out of a disordered stream of observations you first have to notice that something isn't what you expected, and then build a frame that explains the difference. Only after that does information come into being.

3. From apagoge to abduction

The first to sketch this operation was Aristotle. In the Prior Analytics, part of the Organon, he discussed under the name apagoge a kind of inference where the major premise is clear but the minor premise is uncertain. He got as far as the outline and no further. Peirce reread that passage, gave it the name abduction, and stood it up as a third, independent form of inference next to deduction and induction.

What Peirce set up was more than a name. He held that abduction is the only channel through which a new idea enters, and he gave that channel a place inside logic. A logic made of deduction and induction alone tells you how to sort what has already come in, but it can't account for what comes in.

4. Two conditions for abduction

For an operation to count as abduction it has to meet two conditions. First, it has to be able to set up a new space on its own, outside the existing hypothesis space. Picking the most plausible option from choices already on the table does not open a space. Second, the feedback loop that updates the hypothesis against the real world has to be closed. A hypothesis that is posed and then left alone is imagination. Abduction becomes inference only when the loop closes: hold it up against the world, and fix it when it doesn't fit.

The two conditions don't work apart. Open a space without checking it against the world and you can't tell whether the space was right. Have the loop for checking but no way of opening a space, and there's no new hypothesis to check. The first condition makes abduction productive; the second turns what it produces into information.

5. Taking the absence of an answer as the answer

The verse at the top is what I wrote down as the core proposition when I first worked out the intelligence trichotomy. The absence of an answer means a state where uncertainty remains, a game of twenty questions that isn't over yet. Taking that as the answer means treating the leftover uncertainty not as a defect to be removed but as the starting point for the next question.

The definition of abduction is packed into those two lines. If you assume the answer already exists, all that's left is to find it and match it, and no new space opens. Room for a new hypothesis appears only where you accept that there is no answer yet. Abduction is the operation that burns that room as fuel.

The next piece takes up how to judge what abduction opens. If there is an operation that opens spaces, there has to be a criterion for telling whether what it opened is any use. Peirce left that criterion behind too.
