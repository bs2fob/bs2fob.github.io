[Follow-up Q&A]

Q Aren't organs with very high cholesterol demand, like the adrenals and gonads, dependent on the liver's supply up to a certain point?

A Not for everyday supply; LDL serves only as reserve capacity when hormone output has to be pushed to the maximum under stress. The adrenal gland draws on three sources: LDL taken up through LDL receptors and HDL through SR-BI, synthesis inside the cell, and stored cholesteryl esters. In humans the LDL route carries more weight.

Patients with abetalipoproteinemia, who have almost no LDL in the blood, and patients with homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia, who lack LDL receptors, still had normal baseline cortisol; their response was weaker only under prolonged stimulation with ACTH. Trials that pushed LDL below 15 mg/dL with PCSK9 inhibitors found no clinically meaningful change in adrenal or gonadal hormones, and a blunted ACTH response reported in the first months of treatment is read as adaptation.

So what is essential is part of the maximum output in an emergency, and the threshold is low enough that it shows only when LDL receptors are absent altogether, far below treatment targets of 70 or 55 mg/dL. For the adrenal gland, LDL is an extra supply that also serves as reserve capacity for the stress response.

3. A wound is not the way in 10:40 ~ 11:50

What the third chapter secures is that atherosclerosis begins not with a wound in the wall but with retention inside it. The professor's sequence runs: LDL burrows into wounds made by smoking and high blood pressure, resident macrophages eat it until they burst, and the leftover cholesterol piles up. The first two steps part ways with the research.

Particles under about 70 nm cross an intact endothelium by transcytosis, so LDL needs no wound. Most particles that enter slip back out; only those caught by proteoglycans in the wall stay. That retention draws in oxidation and incoming monocytes and leads on to foam cells and a necrotic core.

5. Where the dilemma dissolves 13:10 ~ 16:20

7. Cancer and atherosclerosis

It is already widely known that cancer does not appear out of nowhere one day. Abnormal cells arise steadily even in a healthy body through copying errors, and the immune system finds and removes them. Those scattered cells that come and go are a different layer from cancer tissue, which grows while disrupting the immune system. Atherosclerosis resembles cancer in that it too splits into a layer that forms and is cleared daily and a layer that escapes clearing and builds up.

Where they differ in essence is the location of the cause and the role of immunity. The cause of cancer is mutation inside the cell, and immunity is what holds it back. The material of atherosclerosis is apoB particles floating in the blood, and immunity both clears them and, through inflammation, enlarges the plaque and weakens its cap. That the cause floats in the blood means it can be measured and controlled, which leads to the response in the last chapter.

8. Controlling the apoB count in the blood

What the last chapter secures is that the root response to atherosclerosis is keeping the number of apoB particles floating in the blood low, early and for a long time. apoB, read "apo-B", is short for apolipoprotein B. The prefix apo marks the protein part of a lipoprotein, the part left once the fat is taken away, and the B is the Latin letter, not the Greek beta; lab reports usually print it as Apo B. It is the structural protein the liver fits into each VLDL particle as it assembles it, one per particle, and it stays on as VLDL shrinks into IDL and LDL. Lp(a), which is set by genes, carries the same protein, so counting apoB counts every particle that can enter the artery wall.

LDL cholesterol measures the weight of the cargo; apoB counts the trucks. What enters and is trapped in the wall is the truck, so in studies comparing risk prediction apoB outperformed LDL cholesterol, and European cardiology and atherosclerosis guidelines advise assessing risk by apoB or non-HDL cholesterol (total minus HDL cholesterol) in people with high triglycerides, diabetes, obesity or very low LDL cholesterol. apoB can be measured with an ordinary blood test.

The number is the liver's production minus the liver's clearance (chapter 2). The skeleton of the modern medical approach is to hook a range of mechanisms onto these two handles. Diet and body weight turn mainly the production handle; statins, ezetimibe and PCSK9 inhibitors all turn the clearance handle, and each 1 mmol/L drop in LDL-C cut major vascular events by about 22% (chapter 5). Managing smoking, blood pressure, blood sugar and inflammation is an auxiliary axis that slows the build-up rather than changing particle number.
