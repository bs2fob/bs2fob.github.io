Narratives of Arrival, and Where We Part

Arrangements that look like avatar, handler and governor already exist in many traditions. Looking alike confirms nothing. Only by pinning down where they part ways does the boundary of the governor concept come into focus. There are two criteria for the comparison. First: is the governor treated as a state to be reached, or as a function already available? Second: are space-time coordinates and the boundary between self and other presupposed as facts of the world, or seen as settings for survival? This piece covers the spiritual traditions. They mostly part ways on the first criterion.

1. Advaita Vedanta: sakshin (witness consciousness)

As an observer not subject to arising and passing away, the sakshin is structurally similar. But the sakshin is a goal of practice, to be reached by special means such as meditation and yoga, and it presupposes identification with a metaphysical substance called Brahman. It is a narrative of arrival and a narrative of substance. The governor has nowhere to arrive and sets up no substance to identify with.

2. Gnosticism: pneuma and the three kinds of humans

Gnosticism divides people into three kinds: the hylic (fleshly beings, sunk in matter), the psychic (soul beings, restrained by faith and ethics) and the pneumatic (spiritual beings, carrying pneuma, a fragment of the divine). On the surface this tripartite scheme seems to match avatar, handler and governor. The decisive difference is that pneuma is a substance, trapped in the material world, that must return through gnosis to its home, the pleroma. A narrative of escape and return requires two places: where you are trapped and where you go back to. The trigger sentence says we have never left the here-now, so it denies both places.

The passages describing the pneumatic person as realized only inwardly, behind an outwardly ordinary life, touch in attitude on the definition of the active living cognizer as something that works in everyday life rather than as a special religious state.

3. Sufism: the tripartite nafs, qalb and ruh

Sufi psychology, from Ja'far al-Sadiq on, divides a person into nafs (the lower self, bound to desire and impulse), qalb (the heart, the seat of balance and reflection) and ruh (spirit, the highest layer, closest to God). The form of a hierarchical three-part structure is the same as avatar, handler and governor. Ruh is the endpoint of an ascending path toward nearness to God, so it belongs to the narrative of arrival. The goal of practice, silencing the nafs and revealing the ruh, stands against the premise that the governor is already available.

4. Kabbalah: Luria's nitzotzot

The sixteenth-century theory of Isaac Luria holds that the vessels shattered (shevirat ha-kelim) and sparks of divine light (nitzotzot) were scattered into matter. It is structurally almost identical to the Gnostic idea of pneuma, and it shares a salvation narrative of gathering the scattered light and restoring it (tikkun). It parts from the governor concept for the same reason Gnosticism does.

5. Dzogchen (the Great Perfection): rigpa

In the Tibetan Dzogchen tradition, rigpa (pure awareness, innate knowing) is defined not as a goal newly to be reached but as the ground (gzhi) that has always already been there, and it is suddenly recognized through a master's direct introduction. This has the same narrative structure as the governor's immediate activation through the trigger sentence. They part ways at two points. First, most Dzogchen teachings require the blessing of an authorized master as the necessary route for direct introduction, while the trigger works through recognition of the sentence itself, with no particular authority. Second, rigpa is defined as ultimate reality, the non-duality of emptiness and luminosity, and receives the standing of an ontological absolute. The governor is not the name of a reality but the name of a function: second-order representation.

6. Summing up

The salvation-narrative line of Vedanta, Gnosticism, Sufism and Kabbalah posits a place to escape from and a place to return to, so it runs head-on against the trigger sentence. Dzogchen's rigpa shares the frame of recognition rather than arrival and comes closest, but it cannot shake off the master's mediation and the ontology of an absolute. The governor sets up no place to go, no master to take you there and no absolute to identify with.

With nowhere to go, there is nothing to arrive at.
